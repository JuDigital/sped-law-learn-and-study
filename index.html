Tap a lesson to open it. Each one explains a topic in plain words, with a picture or chart and the test traps to watch for.

★Start here: the special ed process, step by stepThe whole journey on one page
⚡ 30 second versionFind → Help (MTSS) → Refer → Notice + Consent → Test (60 days) → Qualify? → IEP (30 days) → Serve and monitor → Review every year → Retest every 3 years → Transition by 16.

Almost every test question fits somewhere on this path. When you read a scenario, ask yourself: what step are we on?

  1. Child FindThe school actively looks for kids who might need help.
  2. MTSS and interventionsTier 1, 2, and 3 support with data. This can NOT be used to delay an evaluation.
  3. ReferralA teacher or parent asks for an evaluation. Bring your intervention data.
  4. Prior Written Notice + ConsentThe school tells parents in writing. Parents agree in writing.
  5. Evaluation (60 days)Full and fair. Many tools, the right language, no single test.
  6. EligibilityHas one of the 13 disabilities AND needs special ed? Both must be yes.
  7. IEP meeting (within 30 days)The team, including parents, writes goals, services, and placement (LRE).
  8. Consent for servicesParents agree before services start. This is separate from consent to evaluate.
  9. Services + progress monitoringTeach, collect data, and report progress to parents.
  10. Annual review (every year)Check progress and write new goals.
  11. Reevaluation (every 3 years)Does the student still qualify? What do they need now?
  12. Transition (by age 16) then graduationIDEA ends. Section 504 and the ADA keep protecting them as adults.
If parents disagree at any step: they can request an IEE, mediation, a state complaint, or a due process hearing. The student stays put in their current placement while it's settled.

See the full timeline with dates ▾
  • 1954Brown v. Board of EducationEnded racial segregation in schools. Not a disability case, but it set the idea that separate is not equal.Hook: the foundation everything else builds on.
  • 1970Diana v. State Board of EducationA Spanish speaking student was labeled with an intellectual disability after an English IQ test. Students must be tested in their primary language.Hook: Diana = language.
  • 1972PARC v. PennsylvaniaChildren with intellectual disabilities can't be turned away from public school.Hook: PARC opened the door.
  • 1972Mills v. Board of Education of D.C.Children with behavior and emotional needs can't be excluded, and lack of money is no excuse.Hook: Mills = Money is no excuse.
  • 1972Larry P. v. RilesIQ tests placed too many Black students in special ed in California. The court called it biased and discriminatory.Hook: Larry P. = racial bias in IQ tests.
  • 1973Section 504 passesPart of the Rehabilitation Act. Bans disability discrimination in programs that get federal money.
  • 1975EHA passes (P.L. 94-142)The Education for All Handicapped Children Act turned PARC and Mills into national law. It's the law we now call IDEA.
  • 1982Board of Education v. RowleyFirst Supreme Court case on FAPE. Schools must give educational benefit, not the best possible education.Hook: Rowley = the floor.
  • 1988Honig v. DoeSchools can't remove students with disabilities indefinitely for behavior.Hook: Honig = you can't kick them out for good.
  • 1990ADA passes, EHA renamed IDEACivil rights for people with disabilities across daily life. “Person first” name: Individuals with Disabilities Education Act.
  • 2004IDEA reauthorizedStates can't require the IQ discrepancy model for learning disabilities and must allow RTI (response to intervention).
  • 2017Endrew F. v. Douglas CountyRaised the bar. Goals must be appropriately ambitious, with meaningful progress.Hook: Endrew = Expectations raised.
2IDEA vs. Section 504 vs. ADAThe three laws side by side
⚡ 30 second versionIDEA changes the teaching. IEP, 13 categories, ends at graduation.
504 removes barriers. 504 plan, never ends.
ADA is 504's big sibling for everywhere in adult life.

The quick test: Does the student need the teaching changed? That's IDEA. Do they just need barriers removed to access the same teaching? That's 504.

The 6 big principles of IDEA
  • Zero reject: no child with a disability is turned away.
  • Nondiscriminatory evaluation: fair testing, many tools, right language.
  • FAPE: the right education, at no cost, through an IEP.
  • LRE: with general ed peers as much as appropriate.
  • Parent participation: parents are equal team members.
  • Procedural safeguards: parents' rights and ways to settle disagreements.
Accommodation vs. modification
AccommodationChanges how the student learns or shows learning. Same expectations.Extra time, audiobook, preferential seating.
ModificationChanges what the student is expected to learn. Different expectations.Fewer answer choices, shorter test, easier reading level.
3Child Find and MTSSFinding kids and giving help before referral
⚡ 30 second versionSchools must go FIND kids (hide and seek). Tier 1 = all, Tier 2 = some, Tier 3 = few. MTSS can never delay an evaluation.

Child Find means schools must go looking for kids who might have disabilities. That includes private school, homeschooled, homeless, and highly mobile children, from birth to 21.

MTSS is the system of support the school uses for everyone. Think of it as a pyramid:

Tier 3Few students. Intensive, individual help.
Tier 2Some students. Small group, targeted help.
Tier 1All students. Strong core teaching in general ed.
Test trap: MTSS can NOT be used to delay or deny an evaluation. If a parent asks for an evaluation, the school must either ask for consent to evaluate or send Prior Written Notice saying no and why.
Before you refer, document:
  • Which interventions you tried
  • How often and for how long
  • Progress monitoring data (graphs)
  • Work samples and observations
  • Parent input
Two questions to ask yourself first
  • Opportunity to Learn: Did this student actually get good teaching and enough time?
  • Disproportionality: Am I referring this student because of a real need, or because of race, culture, or language?
4Prior Written Notice vs. ConsentWho tells, who agrees, and when
⚡ 30 second versionPWN = school tells the parent, yes OR no (Parents Will kNow).
Consent = parent says yes in writing. Two separate permission slips: one to test, one for services.
Prior Written NoticeSchool ➜ Parent. The school informs.
Required when the school proposes OR refuses to start or change identification, evaluation, placement, or FAPE.Must explain: what, why, what data was used, other options considered, and parent rights.
Informed ConsentParent ➜ School. The parent agrees.
In writing, voluntary, in the parent's native language, and can be taken back.Needed before the initial evaluation, before services start, and before reevaluations.
What if the parent says no?
Parent refuses...What happens
Initial evaluationThe school may use mediation or due process to try to override. It doesn't have to.
Initial servicesThe school cannot override. No services start, and the school is not blamed for denying FAPE.
Revokes consent laterThe school sends PWN and stops services.
Test trap: Consent to evaluate is NOT consent for services. They are two separate signatures.
5Fair evaluation and eligibilityHow the team decides who qualifies
⚡ 30 second versionMany tools, home language, never one test. Eligible = disability in the 13 + needs special ed (two keys). Don't blame the PLATE: Poverty, Language, Absences, Trauma, Environment.
Rules for a fair evaluation
  • Use a variety of tools. No single test can decide anything.
  • Test in the student's native language (Diana).
  • Tests must not be racially or culturally biased (Larry P.).
  • Done by trained professionals.
  • Look at all areas of suspected disability.
Eligibility has two parts. Both must be yes.
Has a disability in one of the 13 categories
+
Needs special education because of it
=
Eligible under IDEA

The 13 categories: autism, deaf blindness, deafness, emotional disturbance, hearing impairment, intellectual disability, multiple disabilities, orthopedic impairment, other health impairment (often ADHD), specific learning disability, speech or language impairment, traumatic brain injury, visual impairment.

Test trap: A diagnosis alone does NOT make a student eligible. A student with autism who is thriving and doesn't need specialized instruction may get a 504 plan instead.
Exclusionary factors: NOT a disability

If the struggle is mainly caused by one of these, the student does not qualify: poverty, trauma, learning English, lots of absences, being unhoused, or lack of good instruction.

6Specific Learning Disability (SLD)The 3 ways it gets identified
⚡ 30 second versionThree ways to spot SLD: a gap (discrepancy), didn't respond to help (RTI), or many sources agree (convergence). States can't force the gap method.

SLD in plain words: a brain based problem with processing language that shows up in reading, writing, spelling, listening, or math, even though the student is otherwise capable. It's the largest IDEA category.

1. IQ Achievement DiscrepancyCompare IQ to achievement. A big gap suggests SLD.Problem: kids have to fall far behind first (“wait to fail”).
2. Response to Intervention (RTI)Did the student respond to good, research based teaching? If not, that points to SLD.Ties directly to MTSS data.
3. Convergence of DataMany sources (tests, teachers, parents, observations) all point the same way.The strongest, fairest decision.
Test trap: Since IDEA 2004, states can NOT require the discrepancy model and MUST allow RTI.
7When parents disagree with the evaluation: IEEThe second opinion right
⚡ 30 second versionParent disagrees → school pays for an outside evaluation OR goes to due process to defend its own. The team must consider the results, not obey them.
  1. Parent disagreeswith the school's evaluation and asks for an IEE at public expense.
  2. School must choose one, without delay:Pay for the IEE, OR file for due process to prove its own evaluation was appropriate.
  3. If the school winsParents can still get an IEE, but they pay for it.
  4. Results come backThe team must consider the IEE results. It doesn't have to follow them.
Test trap: The IEE examiner can NOT work for the district. That's the whole point: independent.
8FAPE: Rowley vs. Endrew F.How good does special ed have to be?
⚡ 30 second versionRowley = LOW-ley, the floor: some benefit. Endrew = ENDed “just enough”: ambitious goals, meaningful progress. Neither one means best possible.
FreeNo cost to parents
AppropriateFits this student
PublicProvided by the public school
EducationSpecial ed + related services
Rowley (1982)The student: Amy, deaf, passing with a hearing aid and tutoring. Parents wanted an interpreter.
The ruling: No interpreter needed. She was already benefiting.
The standard: “Some educational benefit.” Schools don't have to maximize potential.Rowley set the floor.
Endrew F. (2017)The student: Endrew, autism, same goals year after year with almost no progress.
The ruling: That's not enough.
The standard: IEPs must be “appropriately ambitious” and allow meaningful progress in light of the child's circumstances.Endrew raised the floor.
Picture it: Rowley says the school must give the student a car that runs. Endrew says the car has to actually get this student somewhere meaningful.
Test trap: Endrew did NOT overturn Rowley. It built on it. Neither case requires the “best possible” education.
When FAPE is denied, remedies include:
  • Compensatory education: make up services for what was missed.
  • Private school tuition reimbursement: if the district failed to offer FAPE and the parents placed the child privately.
9LRE and the continuum of placementsWhere the student learns
⚡ 30 second versionStart in gen ed. Add walls only when supports don't work. Placement follows the student's needs, not the label, space, or budget.

LRE in plain words: start in general ed. Only move the student out when general ed doesn't work, even with supports and aids.

General ed full time with accommodations and supports
General ed + resource room part of the day
Self-contained class in a regular school
Separate special school or day treatment
Residential placement
Home or hospital
▲ Least restrictive (most included)▼ Most restrictive
Placement rules
  • Decided by the IEP team, including parents.
  • Based on the student's IEP and needs.
  • NOT based on the disability label, available space, cost, or convenience.
  • Reviewed at least once a year.
Test trap: LRE does NOT mean every student must be in general ed full time. It means as much as is appropriate for that student.
10What's in an IEP and who's on the teamThe parts and the people
⚡ 30 second versionPLAAFP (you are here) → goals (where you're going) → services (how you get there). Five required chairs. Never walk in with a finished IEP.
What the IEP must include
  • PLAAFP: where the student is now and how the disability affects learning
  • Measurable annual goals built from the PLAAFP
  • How progress will be measured and when parents get reports
  • Services: special ed, related services, aids, and supports
  • LRE explanation: how much time, if any, away from general ed peers and why
  • State and district tests: with what accommodations, or an alternate test
  • When, where, how often, and how long services happen
  • Transition plan by age 16
How PLAAFP becomes a goal:
PLAAFP: “Jada reads 45 words per minute. Grade level is 90.”
Goal: “By May, Jada will read 80 words per minute with 95% accuracy.”
Required team members
  • Parents
  • At least one general ed teacher
  • At least one special ed teacher
  • District representative (LEA) who can commit resources
  • Someone who can explain evaluation results
  • The student, when appropriate (must be invited for transition)
  • Others the parent or school invite, like related service providers
Test trap: Writing the IEP ahead of time and presenting it as final is predetermination. It violates parent participation. Drafts are fine if the team truly discusses and can change them.
11When parents and schools disagreeFrom least formal to most formal
⚡ 30 second versionTalk → mediate (referee) → state complaint (call the manager) → due process (court) → real court. The student stays put the whole time (freeze tag).
1. Talk it outCall another IEP meeting.
2. MediationA neutral mediator helps both sides agree. Voluntary, free, confidential.
3. State complaintThe state education agency investigates whether the law was broken. File within 1 year.
4. Due process hearingLike a mini court. An impartial hearing officer decides. File within 2 years. A resolution meeting comes first (within 15 days).
5. CourtEither side can appeal the hearing decision to state or federal court.
Least formalMost formal
Stay put: during any dispute, the student stays in their current placement until it's resolved.
Test trap: Mediation can NOT be used to delay a parent's right to a due process hearing.
12Discipline and the 10 day ruleThe flowchart that answers most discipline questions
⚡ 30 second versionUp to 10 days, normal rules. Past 10: services keep flowing + MDR within 10 days. Disability caused it = return + behavior plan. Didn't = normal discipline + services. Guns, drugs, blood = up to 45 days (.45 is a gun).

Background: Honig v. Doe said schools can't remove students with disabilities forever for behavior. These rules came from that.

Student with an IEP breaks the code of conduct
▼
Up to 10 school days: the school can remove the student like any other student.
▼
More than 10 days? That's a change in placement. Services must continue starting day 11.
▼
Manifestation determination within 10 school days.
1. Was the behavior caused by the disability?
2. Was it because the school didn't follow the IEP?
YES to either
Student returns to their placement. Do an FBA and write (or fix) a behavior plan (BIP).
NO to both
Normal discipline like any student, BUT educational services continue.
Special circumstances: weapons, illegal drugs, or serious bodily injuryThe school can place the student in an IAES for up to 45 school days, even if the behavior WAS a manifestation.
Test trap: Services never stop after 10 days, no matter what the manifestation team decides.
13Keeping the IEP currentProgress, reviews, reevaluations, transition
⚡ 30 second versionMonitor always (Fitbit). Review every year (birthday). Retest every 3 (tri). Transition by 16 (Sweet 16).
Progress monitoringOngoingCollect data on goals. Report to parents at least as often as report cards.
Annual reviewEvery yearReview progress, write new goals, review placement.
ReevaluationEvery 3 yearsStill eligible? What's needed now? Not more than once a year unless both agree. Can be skipped only if both agree.
Transition planBy age 16Plan for life after high school.
What goes in a transition plan
  • Measurable goals for after high school: education or training, employment, and independent living (when appropriate)
  • Transition services and courses to reach those goals
  • The student must be invited to the meeting
  • Parents and student are told about rights transferring to the student at the age of majority (18 in most states), at least one year before
Test trap: After graduation, IDEA ends. The student is protected by Section 504 and the ADA, but no one comes looking for them. The adult has to ask for accommodations.
14CEC ethics: the 12 principles in real lifeMatch the scenario to the principle
⚡ 30 second versionMatch the situation to the principle. CAN do it now = competence. GO learn more = growth. Share it OUT = dissemination. See harm = report it. Parents = partners.

The test gives a situation and asks which principle applies, or what an ethical teacher should do. Learn what each one looks like.

PrincipleLooks like
1. Challenging expectationsBelieving the student can grow. Never “he can't because of his disability.”
2. Competence and integrityBeing skilled, honest, and using good judgment.
3. Inclusive participationGetting students involved in school and community life.
4. Work well with colleaguesCollaborating with gen ed teachers, paras, and therapists.
5. Respectful family partnershipsAsking parents for their goals. Respecting culture. Involving them in decisions.
6. Use evidence and dataChoosing interventions based on research and student data.
7. Protect safetyKeeping students physically and emotionally safe.
8. Never do or tolerate harmReporting a colleague who restrains a student inappropriately.
9. Follow and improve lawsFollowing IDEA and policy, and pushing to improve them.
10. Advocate for resourcesTelling admin the students aren't getting their IEP services.
11. Professional organizationsJoining and taking part in groups like CEC.
12. Grow and share knowledgeTaking trainings (growth) and teaching others (dissemination).

15How to answer scenario questionsA method for Section 3 style questions
  1. Who is the student?IEP (IDEA) or 504? Which step of the process are they on?
  2. What right is at stake?Evaluation, consent, FAPE, LRE, discipline, parent participation, confidentiality?
  3. What does the law require?Think timelines, notices, consent, stay put, the 10 day rule.
  4. What would an ethical teacher do?Follow the law, include parents, use data, advocate, protect the student.
Usually the RIGHT answer
  • Follows the IEP and the law
  • Includes parents
  • Uses data and multiple sources
  • Keeps the student as included as appropriate
  • Protects confidentiality
  • Documents and reports concerns
Usually the WRONG answer
  • Delays or waits
  • Leaves parents out
  • Uses one test score
  • Uses money or staffing as an excuse
  • Bases placement on a label
  • Ignores a problem to keep the peace
